# Close LEA's loop from slides to students

The best replacement for DEMO101 is **Microsoft's "AI for Beginners" (MIT License), lessons 1–5, combined with the introductory lessons and quiz bank of its sister curriculum "ML for Beginners" (also MIT)**. Together they give a 4–6 week intro AI/ML/neural-network module in Markdown, with no non-commercial or share-alike strings, so it can sit in a public GitHub repo. If a named university course matters more than a clean licence, CS50 AI or MIT OCW 6.034 work for non-commercial research under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, but that licence then extends to every derived file, including LEA-generated KC models.

For the Teacher module, the recommended strategy is **API-first**. Put roles, course ownership, background ingestion jobs, versioned KC models with immutable granular-objective IDs, and a durable append-only event log that tags every session as real, test or simulated into the FastAPI service before building any teacher screen. Then ship an interim Streamlit admin that calls those endpoints, and move it into a role-based section of the Flutter app once the workflows settle. KC review should support both an in-app table editor and a spreadsheet round-trip. The audit should be tiered by how well each check has been validated. The dashboard should lead with OLI-style mastery bands and DataShop-style learning curves, not usage counts.

Every component of this module has precedent: CTAT-style authoring, LLM-generated KCs that experts prefer about two-thirds of the time, learning-curve-driven KC refinement, and teacher dashboards such as Lumilo and OLI. The research notes found **no published system or study that links LLM-drafted KC models, instructor review, content auditing, learner-evidence dashboards, teacher-led lecture redesign and a preview-as-student sandbox**. They also found no study measuring instructor effort in reviewing LLM-generated KC models. Those two gaps are LEA's most defensible research contribution. A 2026 randomized study of data-driven tutor redesign found process gains but no learning-gain difference, so LEA should claim better teacher decisions and process outcomes, not better test scores.

## Microsoft's MIT-licensed curriculum beats every university course on licence cleanliness

Licence terms matter more than prestige here. Everything committed to the public repo, including chunks, transcripts and KC spreadsheets derived from the source, inherits the source licence's conditions. The notes sort candidates into four tiers, and only the first is free of non-commercial (NC), share-alike (SA) and no-derivatives (ND) clauses. **Microsoft's AI for Beginners carries a standard MIT License** granting rights "to use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense" ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/AI-For-Beginners/main/LICENSE)). It is a "12-week, 24-lesson curriculum" with lessons, quizzes and labs, one Markdown README per lesson ([README](https://github.com/microsoft/AI-For-Beginners)). **ML for Beginners is also MIT** ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/ML-For-Beginners/main/LICENSE)). It adds "52 total quizzes of three questions each" as pre- and post-lesson checks, and is "designed to be flexible… can be taken in whole or in part" ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/ML-For-Beginners/main/README.md)). The Hugging Face NLP, Deep RL and Agents courses are Apache-2.0 ([course LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/course/main/LICENSE)) but are pitched at intermediate learners. Google's Machine Learning Crash Course text is CC BY 4.0 under the Developers site policy ([Site Policies](https://developers.google.com/terms/site-policies)), but no bulk-download route was found, so ingesting it means scraping web pages.

Berkeley CS188 publishes slides with no reuse statement and asks adopters to "talk to us before re-releasing" ([CS188 instructions](https://ai.berkeley.edu/project_instructions.html)).

The Microsoft pairing wins on three counts. First, lessons 01 (Intro and History of AI), 02 (Knowledge Representation / Expert Systems), 03 (Perceptron), 04 (Multi-Layered Perceptron) and 05 (Frameworks and Overfitting) cover DEMO101's current intro-AI, ML and neural-network topics directly ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/AI-For-Beginners/main/README.md)). Second, the Markdown source avoids PDF-extraction noise and goes straight into LEA's Markdown loader. Third, the ML for Beginners quiz bank gives real, human-authored items against which LEA's generated quizzes and item-flaw audit can be benchmarked. A workable six-week mapping, which is the notes' inference, is: week 1 Intro to AI (AI4B L01); week 2 Symbolic AI (L02); week 3 ML basics (ML4B intro and regression lessons plus their quizzes); week 4 Perceptron (L03); week 5 MLPs and overfitting (L04–05); and an optional week 6 on ethics or transformers/LLMs (L18/L20). Three caveats apply. The sketchnotes are credited to "@girlie_mac", and whether they fall under the MIT licence is unverified, so leave the images out. The ML4B quiz JSON format was not inspected, so its import path is untested. Finally, MIT requires keeping the copyright notice, which means shipping `courses/<id>/LICENSE`, an `ATTRIBUTION.md` and the pinned upstream commit.

The main alternative is a course from a named university. **CS50 AI** (Search, Knowledge, Uncertainty, Optimization, Learning, Neural Networks, Language) has per-lecture prose notes and is **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** ([CS50 AI License](https://cs50.harvard.edu/ai/2020/license/)). **MIT OCW 6.034** includes lecture videos, notes, and quizzes and exams with solutions in a single zip ([OCW 6.034](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-034-artificial-intelligence-fall-2010/)). OCW items marked "All rights reserved" are expressly *not* covered by its CC licence and must be stripped out ([OCW Help](https://mitocw.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4414756181403-How-is-all-rights-reserved-content-different-from-the-rest-of-OCW-content)). Both suit non-commercial research. The catch is that any distributed KC model, rewritten note or Whisper transcript derived from them must also be CC BY-NC-SA, and anyone forking LEA inherits that restriction. Only the 2020 CS50 AI licence page could be confirmed, because the current one redirect-looped. For a cross-disciplinary second demo, OpenStax Psychology 2e chapters on Learning, Thinking, Memory, and Motivation and Emotion line up neatly with LEA's own theory base. The fetched preface, however, reads CC BY-NC-SA, while the edition is commonly listed as CC BY 4.0 ([Psychology 2e Preface](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/preface)). Check the PDF copyright page, and assume NC-SA until that is resolved.

## Tutoring research validates every module piece, but only in isolation

Each capability the Teacher module needs has its own literature. What is missing is a published system that joins them.

**Authoring.** Classic ITS authoring cut costs by letting non-programmers demonstrate or template content instead of coding rules. CTAT example-tracing tutors report roughly **1:50 to 1:100 instruction-to-development hours against a historical 1:200–1:300** ([Aleven et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-015-0088-2)). CTAT paired its Behavior Recorder with a test loop in which authors run the tutor as a student and see which solution paths are traced ([ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618912.pdf)). A systematic review of 33 authoring-tool papers found that most tools support design *before* instruction, and that authoring *during or after* instruction with combined human and AI input was only emerging ([Dermeval et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-017-0157-9)). LEA's flow, in which an LLM drafts the model and the teacher corrects it with live learner data, sits in that under-explored space.

**LLM-generated KCs.** LLMs now draft KCs that experts often prefer. In Moore et al., three domain experts per subject **preferred GPT-4 KCs about two-thirds of the time**, yet exact agreement with human tags was only **56% (Chemistry) and 35% (E-Learning)**, improving when the top five suggestions were considered ([Moore et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20526)). Multimodal-model KCs matched or beat human KCs on Additive Factors Model fit across five domains ([Moon et al., EDM 2025](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.long-papers.170/index.html)). Supplying course materials through RAG improved KC quality ([Esiason et al., BEA 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.bea-1.18/)). The practical lesson is to show teachers ranked alternatives and source provenance, not a single answer. These studies generate KCs bottom-up from assessment items. LEA generates them top-down from materials and adds Bloom levels, mastery thresholds and prerequisites, fields the notes found no instructor-validation study for.

**Refining KC models from learner data.** This is the strongest evidence base. Learning Factors Analysis, which searches split and merge operations over KC models, found machine-generated models that **beat the hand-built ones on all 11 datasets** under item-stratified cross-validation ([Koedinger, McLaughlin & Stamper 2012](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED537201.pdf)). Humans interpret the flagged KCs through DataShop learning curves ([Stamper & Koedinger 2011](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-21869-9_46)). Liu and Koedinger then closed the loop: they discovered a better model, validated it on new data, redesigned a classroom tutor and found "significant learning gains relative to a control condition" ([Liu & Koedinger 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1155896.pdf)). A counterweight matters, though. A 2026 randomized crossover with 123 students across 5 schools found **"no significant differences by condition" in learning gains** after a data-driven redesign, although students mastered more skills (7.61 vs 6.03) and spent more productive time ([AIED 2026 redesign study](https://www.xiameng.org/aied26_redesign_tutoring_expII.pdf)).

**Automated content audits.** Their reliability splits sharply by task. LLMs are strong on surface item-writing flaws. SAQUET reports **over 94% detection** of human-identified flaws across five domains ([Moore et al. 2024, SAQUET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20529)). A rule-based method outperformed GPT-4 on the same rubric, 91% versus 79% ([Moore et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08161)). Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash reached κ = 0.897 on negatively worded stems. On judging whether an item aligns with a learning outcome, however, all three models tested reached only **ICC 0.12–0.38**, and on Bloom level κ ≈ 0.42 ([Aydin et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13391911/)). Zero-shot LLM Bloom classification scored about 0.72–0.73 accuracy on a six-class dataset ([arXiv 2511.10903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10903)). LLM methods consistently beat non-LLM methods at mapping objectives to Bloom levels, and vague objective wording hurt both ([Kolagar et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.bea-1.32/)). Accessibility checks against WCAG 2.2 success criteria, such as text alternatives and 4.5:1 contrast, are deterministic ([W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). The QM rubric's Standard 2.4 requires that "the relationship between learning objectives, learning activities, and assessments is made clear" ([QM Standards](https://www.qualitymatters.org/sites/default/files/PDFs/StandardsfromtheQMHigherEducationRubric.pdf)). SUNY's OSCQR is CC BY 4.0 and auto-generates an action plan from ratings ([OSCQR](https://oscqr.suny.edu/about/about-oscqr/oscqr-is-unique/)), a direct template for "audit → recommendations."

**Teacher dashboards.** The evidence here is thinner than their popularity suggests. A LAK '24 review of 38 studies found "no evidence to support the conclusion that LADs have lived up to the promise of improving academic achievement." It also named "the assumption that access equates to usage" as a recurring flaw ([Kaliisa et al. 2024](https://saqr.me/publication/2024-kaliisa-learning-analytics-dashboards-lived-hype/)). The clearest causal win is **Lumilo**, a real-time teacher tool showing per-student flags such as wheel-spinning and hint abuse. Across 286 students it beat business-as-usual (r = 0.21, p < 0.001) and helped lower-pretest students most ([Holstein, McLaren & Aleven 2018](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~bmclaren/pubs/HolsteinMcLarenAleven-StudentLearningBenefitsMixedRealityTeacherAwarnessTool-AIED2018.pdf)). Lumilo, however, is real-time and per-student. LEA's use case is design-time and class-aggregate, which is closer to the close-the-loop literature. OLI's dashboard shows objective → sub-objective (skill) mastery in red, yellow and green bands, with a grey state for students who "have answered too few questions" ([OLI Help](https://olihelp.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/32000030730-learning-dashboard-monitor-student-and-class-progress)). ASSISTments surfaces Common Wrong Answers for review ([ASSISTments blog](https://www.assistments.org/blog-posts/using-common-wrong-answers-for-homework-review)). Khanmigo gives teachers per-student chat summaries and full transcripts ([Khan Academy Help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/29473549307277-What-reports-does-Khan-Academy-offer-teachers-to-monitor-their-students-Khanmigo-use)). The notes found no peer-reviewed evidence that those reports change instruction.

**Chat-log mining.** This is feasible but has not been evaluated as a dashboard feature. The StudyChat dataset of 16,851 utterances from an AI course was coded by GPT-4.1 with **κ = 0.741 for broad and 0.575 for specific dialogue acts** against human coders. Assignment-specific "contextual" questions correlated with worse outcomes, while conceptual questions correlated with better exam scores ([McNichols et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07928v3)). CS50's duck reached 1.8 million queries, yet instructor-side analysis was limited to spot-checking 64 answers ([Liu et al. 2024](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/V1fp0567-liu.pdf)).

**Teacher configuration and preview.** Across LLM-era studies, instructors prioritize grounding the tutor in their own materials and pedagogy over persona. **Ten STEM instructors converged on course material and pedagogical strategy but diverged on personalization and course management**, even across their own courses ([Hou et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00057)). Experienced K–12 chatbot creators struggled most with **analyzing conversational data** ([Yoo, Jin & Kim 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00967)). In a 400-student calculus deployment, subtle **pedagogical misalignment was often worse than factual error**, automated evaluation was unreliable on subjective dimensions, and instructor involvement was essential ([Barghorn et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26679)). Time-poor teachers want to be "kept in the loop" without heavy analysis ([Karademir et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12997)). Together these point to a preview mode that exposes the tutor's pedagogical decisions, not only its answers.

## An API-first build lets one developer ship the module in five phases

The strategy follows from three constraints: a single developer, a Flutter client still under construction, and learner data that feeds published papers. The recommendations below are my inferences from the research notes and LEA's known architecture unless a citation is given.

### Put permissions and data plumbing in the API before any teacher screen

All teacher logic should live behind FastAPI endpoints, so the teacher UI can be swapped later without rework and there is a single permission path. The table lists the foundational changes. The most time-sensitive is the event log. **The current Redis evidence list keeps only the last 50 answers per learner and course, so learning curves and item statistics for every course will be lost unless durable logging starts before those courses run.**

| Change | Why it is needed | Suggested implementation |
|---|---|---|
| **Roles and permissions** | Every account is a student today | Add `role` (student / teacher / admin) to `user:{username}`; a FastAPI dependency checks role per route; admins create teacher accounts; log teacher actions to an audit trail |
| **Course ownership** | Teachers must see only their own courses | `course:{code}:owners` and `course:{code}:staff` sets; every teacher endpoint checks membership |
| **Background job queue** | Ingestion and KC generation are slow and costly | A `job:{id}` hash (status, step, percent, log, cost estimate) plus a worker the UI polls. LEA already runs background work (memory consolidation) as a thread inside the API process, started by `build_services().start_background_work()`, which is the Phase 3 decision for single-PC hosting. So an ingestion worker can be another in-process thread reading a Redis job list, with no new dependency. Celery (no official Windows support) and RQ (whose standard worker needs `fork`) aren't needed. |
| **KC versioning with stable IDs** | Mastery and evidence are keyed by `GO_<week>_<lo>_<n>` | Draft and published versions; **freeze an ID at first publication** and store week and position as fields rather than re-deriving them from the ID; new objectives get the next free ID; split and merge recorded in a lineage map (parent → children); deleted IDs tombstoned, not reused; learner sessions pinned to a published version |
| **Learner-data migration rules** | Edits must not corrupt mastery | Reword keeps the ID and evidence; split copies parent evidence to children as a weak prior; merge pools evidence; delete archives it. Each rule runs as a job with a dry-run report |
| **Durable append-only event log** | Analytics and research need full history | Per-course Redis Stream (`events:{course}`, no MAXLEN trimming, AOF persistence on) with a nightly export to SQLite, DuckDB or Parquet for analysis. Each event records pseudonymous learner ID, **session_type (real / test / sim)**, KC version, mode, GO, LO, item ID, correct, score, question type, attempt number, scaffold level, CL/ZPD/motivation state and retrieved chunk IDs |
| **Sandbox / test accounts** | Teacher previews must not contaminate papers | `is_test` flag on accounts; test sessions written to a separate namespace and **excluded at write time** from CSV research logs, not filtered afterwards; one-click reset |
| **Analytics aggregation** | Dashboards must be cheap and private | Scheduled job computing per-GO aggregates into a cache; small-cell suppression applied before results leave the API |
| **Consent and visibility flags** | Research consent differs from teacher visibility | Per-student notice acknowledgement and a separate research-consent flag; PII stripping before any LLM summarization of chats |

### Start the teacher UI in Streamlit and graduate it into Flutter

| Option | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Role-based section of the Flutter app** | One codebase and design system; "test as student" is a role switch in the same client; no second auth UI | Dense grid editing and charts are harder in Flutter web; competes with finishing the student app |
| **B. Separate admin web app** (e.g., a JS framework with a mature data grid) | Best table and chart tooling; admin code isolated from students | A second codebase and stack for one developer; duplicated auth and styling |
| **C. Interim Streamlit admin** | Fastest to build; Python; a table editor out of the box; already in the stack | Weaker auth and session model; not a long-term product; tempting to bypass the API |

**Recommendation: C now, then A.** Build the teacher workflows in Streamlit, making every call through the new FastAPI teacher endpoints and never directly into `src/services`. Use it yourself to onboard the first courses and the new DEMO101. Once the workflows and data contracts stabilize, rebuild the teacher section inside the Flutter app, where preview-as-student naturally belongs. Option B is only worth its cost if outside teachers become the main users. The Bespoke Bots finding that configuration needs vary per course and per instructor ([Hou et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00057)) argues for discovering the workflow with a cheap UI before committing to a polished one.

### Edit the KC model in a table and a spreadsheet, but let only the API assign IDs

Offer both editing routes, backed by one validation service. The in-app table is where the evidence-backed features go. It should show each objective's **source chunk or slide as provenance**, since RAG grounding improved KC quality ([Esiason et al. 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.bea-1.18/)), and **2–5 alternative phrasings, Bloom levels or prerequisite sets**, mirroring the top-5 improvement in Moore et al. ([Moore et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20526)). It should also show a diff against the published version. The spreadsheet round-trip exists because teachers bulk-edit in Excel. Export with a locked ID column. On import, the API validates unique IDs, existing prerequisite references, no prerequisite cycles, no prerequisite scheduled after its dependant, Bloom values within the enumeration, and thresholds within range. It then shows a change preview before anything becomes a draft. Rows without an ID are treated as new objectives, and the API assigns their IDs. Publishing is an explicit step that triggers the migration dry-run. Logging every edit (field, before, after, time spent) costs little and produces the instructor-editing dataset the literature lacks.

### Tier the audit by how far each check has been validated

The audit should produce **findings, not scores**. Each finding carries a severity, confidence, evidence snippet and a teacher disposition (accept, dismiss, fixed). Dispositions give LEA per-course precision, or false-alarm rates, which matters because LLM judges can be consistent without being valid. Deterministic checks run at ingestion. LLM checks run as a background job.

| Check | Method | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| KC schema integrity (unique IDs, valid references, prerequisite cycles, value ranges) | Deterministic | Needs no validation (inference) |
| Prerequisite ordering (dependant scheduled before its prerequisite) | Deterministic over KC model | The check is exact, but the LLM-generated prerequisites themselves are unvalidated |
| Accessibility: missing alt text, contrast, missing slide titles or headings, scanned PDFs without a text layer, uncaptioned media, broken links | Parser rules against WCAG 2.2 | **Backed**: testable success criteria ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)); maps to QM Standard 8 ([QM](https://www.qualitymatters.org/sites/default/files/PDFs/StandardsfromtheQMHigherEducationRubric.pdf)) |
| Item-writing flaws in generated and teacher questions (negative stems, "all of the above," cueing, longest-answer-correct) | Rules plus LLM, or reuse SAQUET | **Backed**: >94% flaw detection ([SAQUET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20529)); κ = 0.897 on negative stems ([Aydin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13391911/)) |
| Non-measurable objective verbs ("understand," "know," "appreciate") | Lexical | Motivated by vague wording hurting Bloom mapping ([Kolagar et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2025.bea-1.32/)) |
| Bloom distribution per week and course; disagreement between stored and re-classified levels | Second-model re-classification | **Moderate**: ~0.72 accuracy, κ ≈ 0.42 per item; use distributions, label items "suggested" |
| Objective ↔ assessment alignment and Bloom mismatch (e.g., "Analyze" objective tested only by "Remember" items) | Retrieval similarity plus LLM rationale | **Weak**: ICC 0.12–0.38 ([Aydin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13391911/)); show as ranked "likely gaps" |
| Coverage gaps (count and similarity mass of supporting chunks per GO) and support appearing after the assessed week | Embedding similarity, threshold calibrated per course | **Experimental**: no validated method found |
| Redundancy across weeks | Pairwise similarity | Experimental; the teacher decides whether it is intended spiral review |
| Readability and slide text density | Formulas, word and bullet counts | Heuristic; no validated thresholds found |
| RAG answerability probe (can retrieval answer generated probes for each GO?) | Reuse LEA's RAG verification script | Experimental; LEA-specific |
| Course-profile consistency (e.g., code questions generated where the profile forbids them) | Rule over generated items | LEA-specific guard for its known code-question failure mode |
| Factual grounding of generated explanations against source chunks | Claim-to-chunk entailment | Experimental; no course-content validation study found |

### Lead the dashboard with learning curves and mastery bands, not usage counts

The reviews' lesson is that teachers act on metrics tied to a concrete decision. LEA's decision is which concept to retool and how. The default view should therefore be one row per learning objective, expandable to granular objectives, showing a mastery band, a learning-curve verdict, a confusion signal and linked audit findings. A "why" drill-down leads to example errors and paraphrased questions. Suppress any cell with fewer than about five students. That is a common disclosure convention, but the notes found no learning-analytics-specific numeric standard, so justify it in the ethics application. Show evidence counts beside every estimate.

| Metric | What it tells the teacher | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| **Mastery band per LO/GO with grey "insufficient evidence"** | Where the class stands | **Precedent**: OLI production design ([OLI Help](https://olihelp.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/32000030730-learning-dashboard-monitor-student-and-class-progress)) |
| **Learning curve per GO (error rate by opportunity)**: flat means instruction is failing; high but declining means hard but working | Whether the concept is being *learned*, not just whether it is hard | **Strongest**: DataShop and LFA lineage ([Koedinger et al. 2012](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED537201.pdf); [Liu & Koedinger 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1155896.pdf)); requires the event log |
| Item difficulty, discrimination, distractor selection rates | Which questions are broken rather than the concept | Standard psychometrics, but unstable at pilot class sizes; hold back below a minimum N |
| Common wrong answers per GO | Recurring misconceptions to address in lecture | **Practice precedent**: ASSISTments ([blog](https://www.assistments.org/blog-posts/using-common-wrong-answers-for-homework-review)) |
| Tutor struggle: attempts per objective, consecutive-incorrect streaks, YouTube-help triggers | Where scaffolding is not enough | Analogous to Lumilo's wheel-spinning flags ([Holstein et al. 2018](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~bmclaren/pubs/HolsteinMcLarenAleven-StudentLearningBenefitsMixedRealityTeacherAwarnessTool-AIED2018.pdf)), but that evidence is real-time and per-student; aggregate design-time use is **experimental** |
| Chat confusion density per KC with coarse dialogue acts and paraphrased examples | What students ask about and how | **Partly backed**: coarse LLM coding κ = 0.741 ([StudyChat](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07928v3)); as a dashboard, experimental |
| Content hotspots: chunks and slides retrieved during struggling turns | Which slide or page to rewrite | **Experimental**; LEA-specific |
| Aggregate cognitive-load, ZPD and motivation state per GO | Whether difficulty is overload or disengagement | **Experimental and sensitive**; aggregate only, shown with uncertainty |
| Retool priority: triangulates audit flags, learner performance and confusion | The ranked to-do list | **Experimental**; the core contribution |
| KC refinement suggestions (split, merge, re-threshold) with LLM explanation | Whether the model, not the lecture, is wrong | Refinement is backed by LFA (11/11 datasets); LLM explanations of splits are unevaluated |

Crossing mastery deficit with chat-confusion density separates two cases. "Wrong and silent" suggests students do not know they are confused, which calls for a diagnostic explainer. "Asked a lot, then mastered" suggests the material works but is slow, which calls for a clearer slide. Log teacher use of the dashboard (views, drill-downs, dispositions), because access does not equal usage ([Kaliisa et al. 2024](https://saqr.me/publication/2024-kaliisa-learning-analytics-dashboards-lived-hype/)).

### Make "test as student" an instrumented sandbox that never touches research data

A plain student login will not catch the failures that matter. The calculus deployment found that pedagogical misalignment was worse than factual error ([Barghorn et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26679)), and CTAT's test loop worked because authors could see *how* the tutor traced them ([Aleven et al.](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618912.pdf)). The preview should therefore add a **glass-box overlay** that shows, for each turn, the GO the orchestrator mapped to, the current mastery estimate against its threshold, the CL×ZPD cell and scaffolding level, the motivation classification and the retrieved chunks, each linked back to its KC row. Three more pieces complete it: **persona presets** (novice, misconception-holder, disengaged, advanced) that reuse LEA's simulation agents; **an auto-generated probe checklist per week** covering each GO, a hint request, a "just give me the answer" request and an off-topic turn, since hint and verification requests were weak spots in Barghorn et al.; and **one-click reset and re-preview after edits**. Test sessions carry `session_type=test` from the first event and never reach research CSVs.

### A five-phase roadmap sequences data capture before dashboards

### Risks concentrate in data integrity and over-trusting LLM judges

The highest-impact risk is **ID drift**. Re-deriving `GO_<week>_<lo>_<n>` after an edit would silently re-attach mastery to the wrong concept, so IDs must freeze at publication. Second is **research contamination** if test or simulated sessions leak into logs. Tagging at write time is safer than filtering after the fact. Third is **alert fatigue**. With alignment ICC at 0.12–0.38, an unranked flood of LLM findings will teach teachers to ignore the audit, so rank findings, cap the list and measure precision. Fourth, **small-N instability** in class-sized cohorts makes item statistics and learning curves noisy. The grey "insufficient evidence" state is a guardrail, not decoration. Fifth, **affect inferences** (cognitive load, motivation) are sensitive derived data. Keep them aggregate, and follow purpose-limitation and consent practice ([Drachsler & Greller 2016](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2883851.2883893)); unlike Khanmigo's full-transcript model, show no raw transcripts by default. Operationally, background ingestion and LLM audits share **one Windows PC** with live tutoring and OpenAI budgets, so jobs need concurrency limits, cost estimates before launch and off-peak scheduling. Finally, **licence contamination** would turn a public KC model derived from NC-SA material into NC-SA content, which is one more reason the MIT-licensed DEMO101 is the right public default.

## Six gaps give LEA a publishable contribution

The notes repeatedly flag the same missing links. Each gap below is a candidate claim, subject to a targeted novelty search first. Several highly relevant 2026 papers could not be read: "Will It Teach as Intended? How Teachers Configure Educational AI Chatbots" ([arXiv 2609.29993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29993)), the CHI 2026 campus-wide course-tutor platform paper ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791298)) and Chat-LAD ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3698205.3733922)). Any of them could narrow a claim.

| Gap in the literature | Closest existing work | What LEA can claim |
|---|---|---|
| No measurement of **instructor editing behaviour** on LLM-generated KC models (edit rates, time, most-corrected fields) | Expert preference ratings of LLM KCs ([Moore et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20526)) | First field data on reviewing a top-down LLM KC model with Bloom levels, thresholds and prerequisites, against CTAT's 1:50–1:100 baseline |
| No study combining **LLM-drafted KC models + data-driven refinement + instructor acceptance** | LFA and DataShop human–machine discovery ([Stamper & Koedinger](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-21869-9_46)) | A full pre- to post-instruction KC lifecycle |
| No study combining an **LLM content audit with learner data** to recommend lecture or slide revisions | Separate item-quality audits and close-the-loop tutor redesigns | Triangulated retool recommendations |
| No peer-reviewed evaluation of an **LLM-tutor teacher dashboard** measuring changes to instruction; no study of teachers using KC evidence to redesign **lectures or slides** | Lumilo (real-time, per-student); close-the-loop studies redesign the tutor itself ([Liu & Koedinger](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1155896.pdf)) | "Teacher-mediated design-loop adaptivity" |
| No empirical study of a **preview-as-student mode for an LLM tutor** (uptake, issues found, time) | CTAT test loop; Barghorn's benchmark testing | An instrumented glass-box sandbox with issues-found metrics |
| No loop from **chat-confusion clustering → redesign → improvement** | StudyChat offline analysis ([McNichols et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07928v3)) | KC-mapped confusion signals feeding redesign |

The evaluation template already exists in Liu and Koedinger's four steps: discover, interpret, validate on new data, redesign and test. Given the 2026 null result on learning gains, the primary outcomes should be those LEA can measure honestly with class-sized cohorts: instructor minutes per course-week of KC review; the percentage of objectives edited, added or deleted, and which fields changed; audit precision from teacher dispositions; issues found per preview session; SUS for the teacher module; trust before and after preview; and whether retooled GOs show steeper learning curves in the next cohort. The three private courses plus the public DEMO101 give a natural multi-course, multi-discipline sample for the scalability chapter of the extended paper.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the Teacher module. It is not an admin panel bolted onto a tutor. It is the missing human half of a design loop that the ITS community built for tutors, and it has not yet been built for LLM tutors or for teachers' own lectures. That changes the build order. The event log and stable KC IDs are the research infrastructure, not plumbing, and every week they are missing is a week of course evidence that cannot be recovered. It also changes how the audit should be framed. The literature supports LLMs as screeners of surface flaws and as producers of ranked hypotheses about alignment and coverage, never as judges. A teacher disposition on every finding is therefore both the right UX and a new dataset.

The strategic payoff is that LEA's existing strengths line up with the evidence. It has a structured KC model where most LLM tutors expose only prompts, pedagogical state where others expose only answers, and simulation agents that can drive a preview. Pairing them with an MIT-licensed public course lets the whole pipeline be demonstrated and replicated openly. The claim that will survive review is better-informed teacher decisions and a measurable KC lifecycle, not higher test scores.
